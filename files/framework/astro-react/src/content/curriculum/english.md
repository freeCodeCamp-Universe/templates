# Session 1: Learn the Decoder

## 1.1 The Decoder Method

### Welcome to the Course

This course explains its method before teaching any word parts.

> **Welcome to Medical Terminology Decoder.**
>
> You do not need biology, Latin, or Greek. Medical words often look enormous because several small meaning-parts have been joined together. You will learn to hear those parts.
>
> Your goal is not to memorize every word on every card. Your goal is to recognize recurring pieces. If you hear _pericarditis_ and can reason "around plus heart plus inflammation," you have succeeded, even if your first definition sounds awkward.
>
> Use this three-step routine:
>
> 1. Catch the ending. It often tells you what kind of problem or procedure this is.
> 2. Find the body root.
> 3. Add the beginning, then turn the literal pieces into natural English.
>
> Medical words have exceptions. Treat your analysis as a strong clue, not a diagnosis.

### Notation Guide

These are the symbols used throughout the course.

| Notation                | Meaning                                             | Example                             |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| `cardi/o`               | Root plus its usual combining vowel                 | `cardi/o/logy`                      |
| `hyper-`                | Prefix placed before a root                         | `hyper/tension`                     |
| `-itis`                 | Suffix placed after a root                          | `arthr/itis`                        |
| `/o/`                   | A joining sound, usually without meaning of its own | `gastr/o/enter/itis`                |
| CAPITALS in a sound cue | Stressed spoken syllable                            | `cardiology`: car-dee-**OL**-uh-jee |

### The Combining Vowel

The letter `o` connects word parts and follows a few predictable rules.

> The letter **o** is often a connector. It makes word parts easier to say. It usually carries no medical meaning.
>
> Keep it between two roots: `gastr/o/enter/itis`.
>
> Usually drop it before a suffix beginning with a vowel: `hepat/itis`, not `hepatoitis`.
>
> Keep it before a suffix beginning with a consonant: `cardi/o/logy`.
>
> Real medical language contains rebels. Your job is recognition, not policing every spelling rule.

## 1.2 Word Parts

### Four Kinds of Pieces

Every medical word breaks down into a small set of building blocks.

> Think of a medical word as a short instruction.
>
> - The **root** names the main subject: `cardi` means heart.
> - A **prefix** modifies the subject: `peri` means around.
> - A **suffix** tells you the kind of event: `itis` means inflammation.
> - A **combining vowel**, usually `o`, helps the pieces connect.
>
> `peri/card/itis` therefore gives you "inflammation around the heart." Natural medical English calls that inflammation of the sac around the heart.
>
> Start with the suffix because it tells you whether you are hearing an inflammation, condition, blood finding, tumor, pain, disease, test, or procedure.

### `a-`, `an-`

`a-`/`an-` means without, absent, or not (Greek).

> "uh / an." Coach: "At the front of a medical word, _a_ or _an_ often removes something."

Examples: `apnea`, `anoxia`, `anemia`, `anuria`, `anencephaly`.

Also: `asystole`, `aseptic`, `avascular`, `arrhythmia`, `anorexia`.

### `dys-`

`dys-` means bad, painful, difficult, or abnormal (Greek).

> "diss." Coach: "Dys says a normal function has become difficult, painful, or abnormal."

Examples: `dyspnea`, `dysuria`, `dysphagia`, `dysplasia`, `dysrhythmia`.

Also: `dyskinesia`, `dyspepsia`, `dysentery`, `dysfunction`, `dysmenorrhea`.

### `hyper-`/`hypo-`

`hyper-`/`hypo-` mean above or below normal (Greek).

> "HIGH-per / HIGH-poh." Coach: "Hyper goes high; hypo goes low."

Examples: `hypertension`, `hypotension`, `hyperglycemia`, `hypoglycemia`, `hyperkalemia`.

Also: `hypokalemia`, `hypernatremia`, `hyponatremia`, `hyperthermia`, `hypothermia`, `hyperthyroidism`, `hypothyroidism`.

### `-itis`

`-itis` means inflammation (Greek).

> "EYE-tiss." Coach: "When you hear itis, ask what is inflamed."

Examples: `arthritis`, `bronchitis`, `dermatitis`, `encephalitis`, `gastritis`.

Also: `hepatitis`, `meningitis`, `nephritis`, `pancreatitis`, `pericarditis`.

### `-osis`, `-iasis`

`-osis`/`-iasis` mean an abnormal condition or process (Greek).

> "OH-sis / EYE-uh-sis." Coach: "This ending labels a condition; the root tells you which one."

Examples: `cyanosis`, `fibrosis`, `necrosis`, `psychosis`, `thrombosis`.

Also: `scoliosis`, `stenosis`, `atherosclerosis`, `nephrolithiasis`, `cholelithiasis`.

### `-emia`

`-emia` means a condition in or of the blood (Greek).

> "EE-mee-uh." Coach: "Emia moves your attention to the blood."

Examples: `anemia`, `bacteremia`, `hyperglycemia`, `hypoglycemia`, `leukemia`.

Also: `septicemia`, `uremia`, `hyperkalemia`, `hyponatremia`, `hypoxemia`.

### `-oma`

`-oma` means a mass, swelling, or tumor (Greek).

> "OH-muh." Coach: "Oma signals a mass or tumor, but it does not by itself tell you whether the mass is cancer."

Examples: `adenoma`, `carcinoma`, `glioma`, `hematoma`, `hepatoma`.

Also: `lymphoma`, `melanoma`, `myeloma`, `neuroma`, `sarcoma`.

### `-algia`, `-dynia`

`-algia`/`-dynia` mean pain (Greek).

> "AL-jee-uh / DIN-ee-uh." Coach: "Find the body root, then attach the idea of pain."

Examples: `arthralgia`, `cephalalgia`, `gastralgia`, `mastalgia`, `myalgia`.

Also: `neuralgia`, `otalgia`, `nephralgia`, `mastodynia`, `pleurodynia`.

### `-pathy`

`-pathy` means disease or disorder (Greek).

> "PATH-ee." Coach: "Pathy says something is wrong with the named structure; it usually does not tell you the exact cause."

Examples: `arthropathy`, `cardiomyopathy`, `encephalopathy`, `gastropathy`, `hepatopathy`.

Also: `myelopathy`, `nephropathy`, `neuropathy`, `retinopathy`, `vasculopathy`.

## 2.2 Heart and Vessels

### Anatomy Map

This module maps the heart and vessels before working through the word-building cards below.

> You need six facts: not a cardiology course.
>
> 1. The heart is a pump.
> 2. Arteries carry blood away from the heart.
> 3. Veins carry blood back toward the heart.
> 4. Vessels is the umbrella term for those tubes.
> 5. Blood can collect outside vessels as a hematoma.
> 6. Blood can solidify inside a vessel as a thrombus, or clot.

### `cardi/o`

`cardi/o` means heart (Greek).

> "CAR-dee-oh." Coach: "When you hear cardi, put the heart at the center of the word."

Examples: `cardiac`, `cardiology`, `cardiologist`, `cardiomegaly`, `cardiomyopathy`.

Also: `cardiovascular`, `cardiogenic`, `cardiopulmonary`, `electrocardiogram`, `echocardiogram`, `endocarditis`, `pericarditis`, `tachycardia`, `bradycardia`.

### `angi/o`, `vascul/o`, `vas/o`

`angi/o`, `vascul/o`, and `vas/o` all mean vessel (Greek/Latin).

> "AN-jee-oh / VAS-kyoo-loh / VAY-zoh." Coach: "All three point to tubes that carry blood or other fluid; in TV medicine they usually mean blood vessels."

Examples: `angiogram`, `angiography`, `angioplasty`, `angiogenesis`, `vasculitis`.

Also: `angiopathy`, `vascular`, `cardiovascular`, `vasodilation`, `vasoconstriction`, `vasopressor`, `vasospasm`.

### `arteri/o`, `ather/o`; `ven/o`, `phleb/o`

`arteri/o` and `ather/o` mean artery and plaque; `ven/o` and `phleb/o` mean vein (Greek/Latin).

> "ar-TEER-ee-oh / ATH-er-oh / VEE-noh / FLEB-oh." Coach: "Arteries carry blood away; veins carry it back. Ather points to fatty plaque."

Examples: `arterial`, `arteriole`, `arteritis`, `arteriography`, `arteriosclerosis`.

Also: `atherosclerosis`, `venous`, `intravenous`, `venipuncture`, `phlebitis`, `thrombophlebitis`, `phlebectomy`.

### `hem/o`, `hemat/o`

`hem/o` and `hemat/o` mean blood (Greek).

> "HEE-moh / hee-MAT-oh." Coach: "Hem and hemat mean blood, even when the spelling shifts."

Examples: `hematology`, `hematoma`, `hematuria`, `hemolysis`, `hemorrhage`.

Also: `hemoglobin`, `hematocrit`, `hematopoiesis`, `hematemesis`, `hemoptysis`, `hemostasis`, `hemothorax`.

### `thromb/o`

`thromb/o` means clot (Greek).

> "THROM-boh." Coach: "Thromb means a clot or a cell involved in clotting."

Examples: `thrombosis`, `thrombus`, `thrombocyte`, `thrombocytopenia`, `thrombocytosis`.

Also: `thrombolysis`, `thrombolytic`, `thrombectomy`, `thrombophlebitis`, `thromboembolism`.

### `tachy-` / `brady-`

`tachy-` and `brady-` mean fast or slow (Greek).

> "TACK-ee / BRAY-dee." Coach: "Tachy races; brady takes a break."

Examples: `tachycardia`, `bradycardia`, `tachypnea`, `bradypnea`, `tachyarrhythmia`.

Also: `bradyarrhythmia`, `tachysystole`, `bradykinesia`, `tachyphylaxis`, `bradysystole`.

### `endo-`, `intra-`, `peri-`, `epi-`

The next two cards place a problem in space. Only the five active examples below are taught for now; the location families return in later sessions.

`endo-`, `intra-`, `peri-`, and `epi-` mean within, inside, around, or upon (Greek/Latin).

> "EN-doh / IN-truh / PAIR-ee / EP-ee." Coach: "These prefixes place the problem: inside, around, or on a structure."

Examples: `endocarditis`, `endoscopy`, `endotracheal`, `intravenous`, `intracranial`.

Also: `intramuscular`, `pericarditis`, `perinatal`, `epidural`, `epidermal`, `epigastric`, `endocrine`.

### `inter-`, `trans-`, `sub-`, `supra-`

`inter-`, `trans-`, `sub-`, and `supra-` mean between, across, under, or above (Latin).

> "IN-ter / tranz / sub / SOO-pruh." Coach: "Hear the map: between, across, under, above."

Examples: `intercostal`, `intervertebral`, `transdermal`, `transfusion`, `subcutaneous`.

Also: `interstitial`, `transurethral`, `subdural`, `sublingual`, `suprapubic`, `supraventricular`, `suprarenal`.

## 3.2 Brain and Nerves

### Nervous System Map

This session moves from the heart and vessels to the brain and nervous system. Learn six landmarks before working through the individual word-part cards.

> You need six landmarks.
>
> - The **brain** processes and coordinates.
> - The **spinal cord** carries signals between brain and body.
> - **Nerves** branch from the brain and spinal cord.
> - The **meninges** are protective membranes around the brain and spinal cord.
> - The **cranium** is the skull around the brain.
> - **Psych** words concern the mind or mental state, not the physical brain alone.

### `neur/o`

`neur/o` means nerve (Greek).

> "NOOR-oh." Coach: "Neur points to nerves and the nervous system."

Examples: `neurology`, `neurologist`, `neurological`, `neuron`, `neuropathy`.

Also: `neuralgia`, `neuromuscular`, `neurosurgery`, `neurotoxicity`, `neurogenic`.

### `encephal/o`, `cerebr/o`

`encephal/o` and `cerebr/o` both mean brain or cerebrum; `encephal/o` is Greek and `cerebr/o` is Latin.

> "en-SEFF-uh-loh / seh-REE-broh." Coach: "Both point to the brain; encephal is Greek, cerebr is Latin."

Examples: `encephalitis`, `encephalopathy`, `electroencephalogram`, `encephalocele`, `anencephaly`.

Also: `encephalomyelitis`, `meningoencephalitis`, `cerebral`, `cerebrospinal`, `cerebrovascular`, `cerebritis`, `cerebrovascular accident`.

### `crani/o`, `mening/o`

`crani/o` means skull, and `mening/o` means the membranes around the brain and spinal cord (Greek).

> "CRAY-nee-oh / meh-NIN-goh." Coach: "Crani is the skull; mening is the protective wrapping beneath it."

Examples: `cranial`, `craniotomy`, `craniectomy`, `cranioplasty`, `intracranial`.

Also: `craniofacial`, `meningitis`, `meningioma`, `meningeal`, `meningoencephalitis`, `meningomyelocele`.

### `myel/o`

`myel/o` means spinal cord or bone marrow (Greek).

> "MY-eh-loh." Coach: "Myel has two homes: spinal cord and bone marrow. Context decides."

Examples: `myelitis`, `myelopathy`, `myelogram`, `myelography`, `myeloma`.

Also: `myelodysplasia`, `myelosuppression`, `myelofibrosis`, `myelomeningocele`, `poliomyelitis`.

> **Context fork:** `myel/o` can mean spinal cord or bone marrow. `myelitis` usually concerns the spinal cord. `Myeloma` concerns plasma cells in bone marrow. Do not decide until you hear the rest of the case.

### `psych/o`

`psych/o` means mind (Greek).

> "SY-koh." Coach: "Psych points to the mind, mental state, or mental-health treatment."

Examples: `psychiatry`, `psychiatrist`, `psychiatric`, `psychology`, `psychologist`.

Also: `psychosis`, `psychotic`, `psychosomatic`, `psychotherapy`, `psychotropic`.

### `hemi-`, `semi-`, `para-`

`hemi-` means half, `semi-` means partial, and `para-` means beside or abnormal (Greek/Latin).

> "HEM-ee / SEM-ee / PAIR-uh." Coach: "Hemi and semi divide; para usually puts something beside a structure or outside the usual pattern."

Examples: `hemiplegia`, `hemiparesis`, `hemicolectomy`, `paraplegia`, `parathyroid`.

Also: `hemithorax`, `hemisphere`, `semiconscious`, `semipermeable`, `paraumbilical`.

### `-plegia`, `-paresis`

`-plegia` means paralysis, and `-paresis` means weakness or partial paralysis (Greek).

> "PLEE-jee-uh / puh-REE-sis." Coach: "Plegia is paralysis; paresis is weakness or partial paralysis."

Examples: `hemiplegia`, `paraplegia`, `quadriplegia`, `monoplegia`, `ophthalmoplegia`.

Also: `hemiparesis`, `paraparesis`, `quadriparesis`, `monoparesis`, `gastroparesis`.

## 4.2 Airway Map

### The Breath Route

Follow one breath through the airway before meeting the roots that name each stop along the way.

> Follow one breath.
>
> Air passes the **larynx**, or voice box; travels down the **trachea**, or windpipe; enters the branching **bronchi**; and reaches the **lungs**. The lungs sit inside the **thorax**, or chest, and are wrapped by a thin lining called the **pleura**.
>
> That single route explains most roots in this session.

### `pulmon/o`, `pneumon/o`, `pneum/o`

`pulmon/o`, `pneumon/o`, and `pneum/o` mean lung, or lung and air depending on context (Greek/Latin).

> "PULL-mon-oh / new-MON-oh / NEW-moh." Coach: "Pulmon means lung. Pneum can mean lung or air, so use the rest of the word."

Examples: `pulmonary`, `pulmonology`, `pulmonologist`, `cardiopulmonary`, `pneumonia`.

Also: `pneumonitis`, `pneumonectomy`, `pneumothorax`, `pneumoperitoneum`, `pneumoconiosis`, `bronchopneumonia`.

> `pneum/o` is a context root. In _pneumonia_, it points to lung. In _pneumothorax_, it points to air in the chest. If your first guess is simply "lung or air problem," keep listening.

### `bronch/o`

`bronch/o` means bronchial airway (Greek).

> "BRONK-oh." Coach: "Bronch points to the branching airways inside the lungs."

Examples: `bronchial`, `bronchitis`, `bronchiolitis`, `bronchoscopy`, `bronchospasm`.

Also: `bronchiectasis`, `bronchodilator`, `bronchogenic`, `bronchopneumonia`, `bronchorrhea`.

### `thorac/o`, `pleur/o`

`thorac/o` means chest and `pleur/o` means the lining around the lungs (Greek).

> "thor-ACK-oh / PLOOR-oh." Coach: "Thorac is the chest; pleur is the slippery lining around the lungs."

Examples: `thoracic`, `thoracotomy`, `thoracostomy`, `thoracentesis`, `hemothorax`.

Also: `pneumothorax`, `pleural`, `pleuritis`, `pleurodesis`, `pleural effusion`.

### `trache/o`, `laryng/o`

`trache/o` means windpipe and `laryng/o` means voice box (Greek).

> "TRAY-kee-oh / luh-RING-oh." Coach: "Larynx sits above the trachea; both belong to the main airway."

Examples: `tracheal`, `endotracheal`, `tracheitis`, `tracheotomy`, `tracheostomy`.

Also: `tracheobronchial`, `laryngeal`, `laryngitis`, `laryngoscopy`, `laryngectomy`.

### `-pnea`

`-pnea` means breathing (Greek).

> "NEE-uh." Coach: "Pnea is breathing; the prefix tells you what the breathing is doing."

Examples: `apnea`, `dyspnea`, `tachypnea`, `bradypnea`, `orthopnea`.

Also: `hyperpnea`, `hypopnea`, `eupnea`, `platypnea`, `trepopnea`.

## 5.2 Digestive Map

### Digestive Map

Five organs work along a single pathway to digest food, and their word parts often show up together in the same clinical story.

> You need one route and three helper organs.
>
> Food reaches the **stomach**, moves through the **small intestine**, and then through the **colon**, or large intestine. The **liver** processes nutrients and makes bile. The **gallbladder** stores bile. The **pancreas** releases digestive substances and hormones, including insulin.
>
> These organs sit close together, so a dramatic abdominal case may mention several roots before the team knows which organ is responsible.

### `gastr/o`

`gastr/o` means stomach (Greek).

> "GAS-troh." Coach: "Gastr means stomach, not the entire abdomen."

Examples: `gastric`, `gastritis`, `gastroscopy`, `gastrectomy`, `gastrostomy`.

Also: `gastroenteritis`, `gastroenterology`, `gastroparesis`, `gastralgia`, `nasogastric`.

### `enter/o`, `col/o`, `colon/o`

`enter/o`, `col/o`, `colon/o` mean intestine, with `col/o` and `colon/o` narrowing to the colon specifically (Greek).

> "EN-ter-oh / KOH-loh." Coach: "Enter usually means intestine; col or colon narrows you to the colon."

Examples: `enteritis`, `enteropathy`, `enteric`, `enteroscopy`, `gastroenteritis`.

Also: `enterocolitis`, `colitis`, `colonoscopy`, `colectomy`, `colorectal`, `colostomy`, `megacolon`.

### `hepat/o`

`hepat/o` means liver (Greek).

> "heh-PAT-oh." Coach: "Hepat always brings the liver into the picture."

Examples: `hepatic`, `hepatitis`, `hepatology`, `hepatologist`, `hepatomegaly`.

Also: `hepatoma`, `hepatotoxic`, `hepatocellular`, `hepatorenal`, `hepatobiliary`.

### `pancreat/o`, `cholecyst/o`, `chol/e`

`pancreat/o` means pancreas; `cholecyst/o` and `chol/e` point to the gallbladder and bile (Greek).

> "PAN-kree-at-oh / koh-lee-SIST-oh / koh-lee." Coach: "Pancreat is pancreas; chole points to bile and cholecyst to the gallbladder."

Examples: `pancreatic`, `pancreatitis`, `pancreatectomy`, `pancreatography`, `pancreaticoduodenectomy`.

Also: `cholecystitis`, `cholecystectomy`, `cholelithiasis`, `cholecystogram`, `cholecystostomy`.

### `-rrhea`, `-rrhage`, `-rrhagia`

`-rrhea`, `-rrhage`, and `-rrhagia` mean flow or discharge, or heavy, often uncontrolled flow (Greek).

> "REE-uh / rij / RAY-jee-uh." Coach: "These endings describe flow. Rrhage and rrhagia imply heavy or uncontrolled flow."

Examples: `diarrhea`, `rhinorrhea`, `otorrhea`, `amenorrhea`, `hemorrhage`.

Also: `leukorrhea`, `galactorrhea`, `bronchorrhea`, `menorrhagia`, `metrorrhagia`.

> Double `r` endings look intimidating because Greek pieces changed spelling as they joined. Listen for the final sound: `-rrhea` is flow or discharge; `-rrhage`/`-rrhagia` is heavy, often uncontrolled flow.

### Guest Word Parts

Two useful pieces appear in digestive terms but are not large enough for separate core cards:

- `lith/o` means stone.
- `-emesis` means vomiting.

Therefore, `chole/lith/iasis` is a bile or gallstone condition, and `hemat/emesis` is vomiting blood.

## 6.2 Kidneys and Glands

### Kidney and Endocrine Map

> The **kidneys** filter the blood and produce urine. Urine travels through the urinary tract and is stored in the **bladder**. Greek-rooted words often use `nephr/o`; Latin-rooted words often use `ren/o`.
>
> The **endocrine system** is a network of glands that release hormones. The **thyroid** is one of those glands. Sugar terms often use `glyc/o`; blood-sugar terms combine it with `-emia`.
>
> You do not need the hormone pathways. You need to hear which fluid, organ, amount, or size is changing.

### `nephr/o`, `ren/o`

`nephr/o` and `ren/o` both mean kidney; `nephr/o` is Greek and `ren/o` is Latin.

> "NEFF-roh / REE-noh." Coach: "Nephr is Greek and ren is Latin; both mean kidney."

Examples: `nephrology`, `nephrologist`, `nephritis`, `nephropathy`, `nephrectomy`.

Also: `nephrolithiasis`, `nephrotoxicity`, `renal`, `renal failure`, `hepatorenal`.

### `ur/o`

`ur/o` means urine or the urinary tract (Greek).

> "YOOR-oh." Coach: "Ur points broadly to urine and the urinary system."

Examples: `urology`, `urologist`, `urinary`, `uremia`, `urosepsis`.

Also: `uropathy`, `urography`, `urodynamics`, `urogenital`, `urothelial`.

### `cyst/o`

`cyst/o` means the bladder or a fluid-filled sac (Greek).

> "SIST-oh." Coach: "Cyst can mean the urinary bladder or a sac; context tells you which."

Examples: `cystitis`, `cystoscopy`, `cystoscope`, `cystectomy`, `cystostomy`.

Also: `cystogram`, `cystography`, `cystocele`, `cystolithiasis`, `cystic`.

### `glyc/o`, `glycem-`

`glyc/o` and `glycem-` mean sugar or glucose (Greek).

> "GLY-koh / gly-SEEM." Coach: "Glyc points to sugar; with emia, it becomes sugar in the blood."

Examples: `glucose`, `glycemia`, `hyperglycemia`, `hypoglycemia`, `glycosuria`.

Also: `glycogen`, `glycolysis`, `glycoprotein`, `glycated hemoglobin`, `glycemic`.

### `aden/o`, `thyr/o`, `thyroid/o`, `endocrin/o`

`aden/o` means gland, `thyr/o` and `thyroid/o` name the thyroid gland, and `endocrin/o` names the hormone-secreting system (Greek).

> "AD-en-oh / THY-royd-oh / EN-doh-krin-oh." Coach: "Aden means gland; thyr and thyroid name one major gland; endocrine names the hormone system."

Examples: `endocrine`, `endocrinology`, `endocrinologist`, `endocrinopathy`, `thyroiditis`.

Also: `thyroidectomy`, `hyperthyroidism`, `hypothyroidism`, `adenoma`, `adenopathy`, `adenocarcinoma`, `thyroid`.

### `-uria`

`-uria` means urine or a urination condition (Greek).

> "YOOR-ee-uh." Coach: "Uria points to urine; the beginning tells you what is present or how much is produced."

Examples: `hematuria`, `proteinuria`, `polyuria`, `oliguria`, `anuria`.

Also: `dysuria`, `glycosuria`, `ketonuria`, `bacteriuria`, `nocturia`.

### `-penia`, `-cytosis`, `-megaly`

`-penia`, `-cytosis`, and `-megaly` change a quantity or a size: too few, increased cells, or enlargement (Greek).

> "PEE-nee-uh / sigh-TOH-sis / MEG-uh-lee." Coach: "These endings change quantity or size."

Examples: `leukopenia`, `thrombocytopenia`, `neutropenia`, `pancytopenia`, `leukocytosis`.

Also: `thrombocytosis`, `erythrocytosis`, `cardiomegaly`, `hepatomegaly`, `splenomegaly`.

## 7.2 Bones, Joints, and Muscles

### Body Structure Map

This session moves from internal organs to the musculoskeletal system and skin. The structures involved are bones, joints, muscles, vertebrae, and skin.

> **Bones** provide rigid structure. **Joints** are places where bones meet. **Muscles** pull on structures to create movement. The **vertebrae** are the bones of the spinal column. **Skin** covers and protects the body.
>
> Keep two similar sounds separate:
>
> - `my/o` means muscle.
> - `myel/o` means spinal cord or bone marrow.
>
> One extra syllable can move the meaning to a different system.

### `oste/o`

`oste/o` means bone (Greek).

> "OSS-tee-oh." Coach: "Oste means bone."

Examples: `osteoarthritis`, `osteoporosis`, `osteomyelitis`, `osteotomy`, `osteopathy`.

Also: `osteoblast`, `osteoclast`, `osteosarcoma`, `osteopenia`, `osteogenesis`.

### `arthr/o`

`arthr/o` means joint (Greek).

> "AR-throh." Coach: "Arthr points to a joint."

Examples: `arthritis`, `arthralgia`, `arthropathy`, `arthroscopy`, `arthroplasty`.

Also: `arthrocentesis`, `arthrodesis`, `osteoarthritis`, `polyarthritis`, `hemarthrosis`.

### `my/o`, `muscul/o`

`my/o` and `muscul/o` both mean muscle (Greek and Latin).

> "MY-oh / MUS-kyoo-loh." Coach: "My and muscul mean muscle; do not confuse my with myel."

Examples: `myalgia`, `myopathy`, `myositis`, `myocardial`, `myometrium`.

Also: `myoma`, `electromyography`, `polymyositis`, `muscular`, `musculoskeletal`.

### `spondyl/o`, `vertebr/o`

`spondyl/o` and `vertebr/o` both mean vertebra or spine (Greek and Latin).

> "SPON-dih-loh / VER-teh-broh." Coach: "Both point to the spinal column's bones."

Examples: `spondylitis`, `spondylosis`, `spondylolisthesis`, `spondylopathy`, `spondylodiscitis`.

## 8.2 Cells and Disease

### Cell-to-Disease Map

Cells combine to form tissues, and abnormal new growth is a neoplasm. A tumor can be benign or malignant; cancer is malignant disease, and cancer that has spread to another site is metastatic.

> Pathology studies disease and examines cells and tissues. Necrosis means tissue death. Sepsis is a dangerous whole-body response to infection; it should never be reduced to the casual phrase "blood infection." The immune system and white blood cells help defend against microbes such as bacteria and viruses.

### `onc/o, carcin/o`

`onc/o` and `carcin/o` mean tumor or cancer (Greek).

> "ON-koh / car-SIN-oh." Coach: "Onc is the cancer specialty; carcin points more directly to cancer."

Examples: `oncology`, `oncologist`, `oncologic`, `oncogene`, `oncogenesis`.

Also: `carcinoma`, `carcinogen`, `carcinogenic`, `adenocarcinoma`, `carcinomatosis`.

### `cyt/o, hist/o`

`cyt/o` means cell and `hist/o` means tissue (Greek).

> "SIGH-toh / HISS-toh." Coach: "Cyt is a cell; hist is tissue made from cells."

Examples: `cytology`, `cytoplasm`, `cytotoxic`, `cytopenia`, `leukocyte`.

Also: `erythrocyte`, `histology`, `histopathology`, `histochemistry`, `histocompatibility`.

### `path/o`

`path/o` means disease (Greek).

> "PATH-oh." Coach: "Path names disease itself or the study and development of disease."

Examples: `pathology`, `pathologist`, `pathological`, `pathogen`, `pathogenic`.

Also: `pathogenesis`, `pathophysiology`, `neuropathy`, `cardiomyopathy`, `nephropathy`.

### `necr/o, seps/o, septic/o`

`necr/o` means tissue death, and `seps/o` and `septic/o` signal infection or putrefaction (Greek).

> "NEK-roh / SEP-soh / SEP-tik-oh." Coach: "Necr means dead tissue. Seps and septic signal dangerous infection language."

Examples: `necrosis`, `necrotic`, `necrotizing`, `osteonecrosis`, `avascular necrosis`.

Also: `sepsis`, `septic`, `septicemia`, `aseptic`, `urosepsis`.

### `leuk/o, lymph/o, immun/o`

`leuk/o` means white, `lymph/o` means lymph, and `immun/o` means immune defense (Greek/Latin).

> "LOO-koh / LIM-foh / im-YOON-oh." Coach: "These families cluster around white blood cells, lymph, and immune defense."

Examples: `leukocyte`, `leukemia`, `leukocytosis`, `leukopenia`, `lymphoma`.

Also: `lymphocyte`, `lymphadenopathy`, `lymphatic`, `lymphedema`, `immunology`, `immunodeficiency`, `immunosuppression`, `autoimmune`.

### `bacteri/o, vir/o`

`bacteri/o` means bacterium and `vir/o` means virus (Greek/Latin).

> "bak-TEER-ee-oh / VEER-oh." Coach: "These roots tell you which kind of microbe is involved."

Examples: `bacterium`, `bacterial`, `bacteremia`, `bacteriology`, `antibacterial`.

Also: `bactericidal`, `bacteriuria`, `virus`, `viral`, `viremia`, `virology`, `antiviral`.

### `anti-`

`anti-` means against or counteracting (Greek).

> "AN-tie." Coach: "Anti tells you what a treatment acts against."

Examples: `antibiotic`, `anticoagulant`, `antiemetic`, `antihypertensive`, `antipyretic`.

Also: `antiplatelet`, `antipsychotic`, `anticonvulsant`, `anti-inflammatory`, `antitoxin`.

### `neo-, meta-`

`neo-` means new, and `meta-` means change, beyond, or spread (Greek).

> "NEE-oh / MET-uh." Coach: "Neo means new. Meta often means change or beyond; in cancer dialogue, metastasis means spread to another site."

Examples: `neoplasm`, `neoplastic`, `neoplasia`, `neoadjuvant`, `neovascularization`.

Also: `metastasis`, `metastatic`, `metabolism`, `metaplasia`, `metacarpal`.

> `-oma` means mass, swelling, or tumor. A hematoma is collected blood. An adenoma is usually a benign glandular tumor. A carcinoma is malignant. The rest of the word and the case determine which.

## 10.2 Procedures and Specialties

### Procedure Endings

Procedure endings answer two questions: what action is happening (removal, incision, opening, viewing, recording, sampling, breaking, repairing, or stopping), and where it is happening, which the root tells you. Do not translate every procedure as "surgery." The ending gives a more precise picture.

### `-ectomy`

`-ectomy` means surgical removal (Greek).

> "ECK-tuh-mee." Coach: "Ectomy takes something out."

Examples: `appendectomy`, `cholecystectomy`, `colectomy`, `craniectomy`, `gastrectomy`.

Also: `hysterectomy`, `mastectomy`, `nephrectomy`, `pneumonectomy`, `thyroidectomy`.

### `-otomy`, `-stomy`

`-otomy` means incision into, and `-stomy` means a surgically created opening (Greek).

> "OT-uh-mee / STOW-mee." Coach: "Tomy cuts into; stomy makes an opening. Do not swap them."

Examples: `craniotomy`, `laparotomy`, `thoracotomy`, `tracheotomy`, `fasciotomy`.

Also: `colostomy`, `gastrostomy`, `ileostomy`, `tracheostomy`, `urostomy`.

### `-scope`, `-scopy`

`-scope` means a viewing instrument, and `-scopy` means the act of visual examination (Greek).

> "skohp / SKOH-pee." Coach: "The scope is the tool; scopy is the act of looking."

Examples: `bronchoscope`, `bronchoscopy`, `colonoscope`, `colonoscopy`, `cystoscope`.

Also: `cystoscopy`, `endoscope`, `endoscopy`, `laparoscope`, `laparoscopy`.

### `-gram`, `-graphy`

`-gram` means a record or image, and `-graphy` means the process of recording (Greek).

> "gram / GRAF-ee." Coach: "A gram is the result; graphy is the process."

Examples: `angiogram`, `angiography`, `electrocardiogram`, `electrocardiography`, `electroencephalogram`.

Also: `electroencephalography`, `mammogram`, `mammography`, `myelogram`, `myelography`.

### `-centesis`, `-opsy`

`-centesis` means a puncture to withdraw fluid, and `-opsy` means viewing or examining tissue (Greek).

> "sen-TEE-sis / OP-see." Coach: "Centesis takes out fluid with a puncture; opsy examines what was seen or sampled."

Examples: `amniocentesis`, `arthrocentesis`, `paracentesis`, `pericardiocentesis`, `thoracentesis`.

Also: `biopsy`, `autopsy`, `endomyocardial biopsy`, `needle biopsy`, `excisional biopsy`.

### `-lysis`, `-plasty`, `-stasis`

`-lysis` means to break apart, `-plasty` means repair or reshape, and `-stasis` means to stop or hold steady (Greek).

> "LYE-sis / PLAS-tee / STAY-sis." Coach: "Lysis breaks, plasty reshapes, stasis stops or steadies."

Examples: `thrombolysis`, `hemolysis`, `dialysis`, `adhesiolysis`, `angioplasty`.

Also: `arthroplasty`, `cranioplasty`, `rhinoplasty`, `hemostasis`, `homeostasis`.

### `-logy`, `-logist`

`-logy` means a specialty or field of study, and `-logist` means the specialist (Greek).

> "OL-uh-jee / OL-uh-jist." Coach: "Logy names the field; logist names the specialist."

Examples: `cardiology`, `cardiologist`, `neurology`, `neurologist`, `oncology`.

Also: `oncologist`, `pathology`, `pathologist`, `radiology`, `radiologist`.

### Listening Traps

Some whole forms sound alike or carry more than one meaning depending on context.

- **"sat" versus "stat"**: "His sat is 88" refers to oxygen saturation. "Get CT stat" means immediately.
- **"epi"**: In a drug order, this often means epinephrine. Inside _epidural_ or _epigastric_, `epi-` means upon/above.
- **"MI"**: In cardiac dialogue, myocardial infarction/heart attack. Do not assume every two-letter abbreviation is universal.
- **"PE"**: Often pulmonary embolism in emergency dialogue; it can mean physical examination elsewhere.
- **"negative"**: "Troponin is negative" means the marker was not detected/elevated by that test, not that every heart problem is excluded.
- **"stable"**: A patient can be stable and still be critically ill.
- **"rule out"**: "Rule out stroke" means stroke remains under investigation.
- **"shock"**: The circulatory condition and an electrical defibrillator shock are different meanings.
